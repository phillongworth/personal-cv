Better Care Fund

Senior Manager, Kirklees Council · 2017 – 2023

Overview

I managed the £50m+ Better Care Fund: the pooled health and social care budget that councils and the NHS share locally, signed off by the Health and Wellbeing Board. I looked after the plan, the partnership agreement and the reporting to government, working with the NHS to keep it on track against the national measures on admissions, discharge and independence at home.

What the fund is

The Better Care Fund was announced in 2013 and started in 2015/16. The idea is simple: instead of the NHS and the Council each spending their own money in their own way, they put a share into one local pot and agree together how to use it to help people, especially older and disabled people, stay well and independent at home.

In Kirklees the pot is a formal pooled fund. A partnership board of senior commissioning and finance people from the Council and both local clinical commissioning groups (CCGs) runs it, and the Health and Wellbeing Board signs off the plan. Government tracks progress against a small set of national measures, including how many people get home safely after hospital and how many older people move into long-term residential care.

The money

In 2016/17 the Kirklees pot was £30.8m, including the whole community equipment service. From 2017 the government added the improved Better Care Fund: extra grant for adult social care that had to be pooled into the same plan. Put together:

  • 2017/18: £44.9m (£35.8m Better Care Fund plus £9.1m improved fund)
  • 2018/19: £49.0m (£36.6m Better Care Fund plus £12.4m improved fund)
  • 2019/20: a further £15.4m of improved funding already announced, with the main allocation still to be confirmed

What it paid for

The 2017–19 plan funded eight schemes:

  • Equipment and adaptations: community equipment, a handyperson scheme, assistive technology and home adaptations, so people can stay in their own homes
  • Intermediate care and reablement: helping people regain their independence after hospital, or avoid going in at all
  • Carers support: breaks, advice, emotional support and advocacy
  • Continuing care: jointly funded packages for people eligible for NHS continuing healthcare
  • Protecting social care: care packages for people with the highest needs
  • Eye clinic liaison officers: support for people with sight loss
  • Mental health contracts: voluntary sector services for adults with mental health needs
  • Supporting the voluntary sector: including the “Better in Kirklees” social prescribing service

The extra social care money went on ten more schemes. They included reviewing care packages so they were the right size, electronic monitoring of home care visits, extra support for care homes taking people with more complex needs, help for care providers to recruit and develop staff, more reablement capacity, and a remodelled hospital avoidance and discharge service.

What I did

  • Developed the Kirklees Better Care Fund Plan for 2017–19, which passed the national assurance process in October 2017
  • Took the plan and the local proposals to the Health and Wellbeing Board, and reported on it to the Council's Health and Adult Social Care Scrutiny Panel
  • Kept the Board updated on how the fund was performing against the national measures
  • Helped reshape the fund for 2017–19: fewer schemes, more of them mainly or fully funded from the pot, and new areas such as continuing care brought in, phased over two years rather than changed overnight

How it was going

The March 2017 update to the Board gave a mixed picture for 2016/17:

  • Residential and nursing care admissions (older people): ahead of plan, with 208 admissions against 260 at week 40
  • Delayed discharges from hospital: heading for a 34.4% fall in delayed days, against a local target of 18% (the national expectation was about 40%), mostly thanks to better data
  • Emergency admissions: slightly above plan, so the schemes weren't yet having the effect we wanted
  • Older people still at home 91 days after reablement: 90.4% against a plan of 94.1%
  • Dementia diagnosis (a local measure): 66.8% against a plan of 71%

In response, the partnership board did a detailed review of emergency admissions in September 2016. It concluded the plan already covered the approaches the evidence supports, so the priority was delivering them well rather than adding new ones. A new data system, CareTrak, now linked social care records to hospital activity, which gave much better insight into what was really happening.

Why it isn't straightforward

A national review in 2017 found that the fund's first year fell short of its targets on emergency admissions and delays leaving hospital. Joining up health and social care is hard, and it depends on organisations with different budgets and priorities agreeing to move together.
